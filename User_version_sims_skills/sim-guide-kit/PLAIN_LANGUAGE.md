# Plain-language rules for student-facing text

These rules apply to every word a student reads or hears: the landing card, the Simulation guide (cards and narration), the Where-to-start card, the Guided Inquiry (headings, text, choices, feedback), the Physics sheet, labels and tooltips.

The physics is already hard. The words around it must add no extra load.

## The voice
Write like a teaching assistant at the lab bench talking to a first-year student. Be plain, direct and friendly. Say "you". Contractions are fine. Don't perform, and don't sell.

## Keep
- **Standard physics vocabulary**, even when it started as a metaphor: light clock, worldline, wavefront, light cone, proper time, potential well, energy level, field line, spin up/down, tunnelling. Test: would a physics lecturer say it in a lecture? If yes, keep it.
- **The exact on-screen names** of controls, readouts and objects.
- **Symbols and numbers**, unchanged.

## Remove and replace with plain words
1. **Decorative metaphor and personification.** Words like ghost, race, racers, hugs, chases, rides aboard, forgets, quietly replaced, trustworthy, story, journey, unlock, reveal, magic, secret, mystery, twist, dance, paint, compounding (outside finance), "falls behind" (say "lags by X"). Name the real thing instead: "the dashed formula bob", "the comparison", "is 0.2% slower per swing".
2. **Openers and closers.** Here's…, Let's…, Now for…, Meet the…, Over to you, One last question, Time to…, Ready?, Great!, Spot on, Exactly. Start with the content.
3. **Hype and warm adjectives.** Gentle, neat, big, beautiful, stunning, elegant, simple, really, actually, very, quite, just, fancy, "the big one".
4. **Slogans and mirrored phrases.** "One photon, one tick", "Same photon, two views". Write a plain sentence instead.
5. **Teasers and rhetorical questions.** Only Guided Inquiry prediction prompts may ask a question, and they ask it directly.
6. **Dash asides.** Avoid "—" and "–" in prose; use a full stop or a comma. Numeric ranges such as "5–90°" are fine.
7. **Hedging and padding.** "It's a very reasonable answer, though…", "it's a fair guess, but…". Feedback states what is right and why, in one or two sentences.

## Shape
- One idea per sentence. Aim for 18 words or fewer; never more than 25.
- At most 3 sentences per card paragraph.
- A card that names several controls or parts puts **each item on its own line**.
- **Control cards say what each control changes in the simulation**, and point at that part of the scene. Never just restate what the student can already read on the control: its name with its range or current value ("Slit width a: 100 to 500 nm"). Group controls by the part of the scene they change.
- Use everyday words. Use a technical term only when it is the point, and define it once.
- Feedback starts with its verdict ("Correct." / "Not quite.") only where the sim does not add one itself.

## Narration (spoken)
- The voice **talks the student through the card**; it does not read the card word for word. It says the same thing in a natural spoken way, with small connecting words, "you", and pointers to where things are ("on the left", "in the top bar").
- Same rules: no metaphor, no hype, no openers like "Here's" or "Let's", no slogans.
- Symbols are spoken as words ("theta zero", "two pi root L over g").
- Keep it short: about 1.3× the card text at most.

## Examples
| Not this | This |
|---|---|
| A light clock rides aboard a moving train — change the train's speed to compare one tick timed on board with the same tick seen from the platform. | A light clock sits inside a moving train. Change the train's speed and compare one tick timed on the train with the same tick timed from the platform. |
| Meet the ghost race | The real pendulum and the formula |
| The ⚠ ghost hugs the real bob — it falls behind by only ~0.2% per lap. | The dashed formula bob stays close to the real bob. It lags by about 0.2% per swing. |
| Now for the big race. | Both bobs are released again at 90°. |
| Not quite, though it's a very reasonable answer — it's exactly what T = 2π√(L/g) promises… | Not quite. T = 2π√(L/g) has no θ₀ in it, so it predicts the same time. That formula assumes sin θ ≈ θ, which only holds for small angles. |
| Help, whenever you want it | Getting help |
| Over to you. Press Play, up in the top bar, to release the bob. | Press Play to release the bob. |
